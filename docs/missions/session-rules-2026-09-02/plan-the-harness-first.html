Session Rules: the plan, harness first

Written after getting off track. The order here is deliberate: find out what your real problems need BEFORE building anything else, because four of your own cases were just checked against what exists and only one of them fits.

1. What rules are for

Your words on the epic: "the user should be able to set up rules: when you see this message in the terminal window, do this." The point is agents that keep going without you.

So the measure of this feature is not whether the machinery is elegant. It is whether the things that actually stop your sessions get handled. That is what the harness is for.

2. Your four real cases, against what exists

These are the ones you named, including two that happened last night.

One of your four fits. That is the finding that should drive everything else, and it is exactly what a harness would have told us before any of this got built. The three misses are not bugs - they are three different capabilities the design never had: a clock, a sequence of steps reaching past the screen, and an action that is not typing.

On the red one - my honest read

I think that one is mostly a product fix, not a rule. DevThrottle already decides a session's colour centrally, and "this turn ended with a report, not a question" is a judgement about the screen - the same kind of judgement a rule makes. So the right home is that decision, made better, for everyone, rather than every user writing a rule to undo a wrong colour. Where a rule earns its place is the exception: "for MY architect sessions, a report is not something I need." It belongs in the harness either way, because it is the case that tells us whether the action vocabulary needs to reach beyond typing.

3. The harness

This is the thing to build first, and nothing else should be built until it says what is needed.

What a case is

One case is a real situation, captured rather than imagined, with the answer written down:

situation   what you would say when writing the rule, in your words
screen      the REAL terminal screen, captured from a real session
expect      act | decline
type        the exact text that should be typed, when the answer is act
why         one line - why this case exists, especially the negatives
shape       type-now | wait-then-type | several-steps | change-the-display

The screens must be real. I wrote six screens myself this evening and both models passed all six - which tells us almost nothing, because I wrote them to be passable. Our own repository already set the bar: the dictation judge was measured over 21 live cases and four of the five models tried failed it. Invented screens are how a harness certifies a feature that does not work.

Half the cases must be negatives

A rule only reaches the model when its trigger words are already on the screen. So the only thing standing between a rule and a wrong keystroke is the answer to "is this really that situation". A harness of positives would pass a model that says yes to everything, which is the worst possible outcome: it types into your sessions on a screen it misread. Every rule shape needs its screens where the words are present and the answer is still no - the words in documentation being read, in code just written, in a report about a different session.

Where the cases come from

  • Your fleet, live. The Gateway can already read any session's screen. A capture command that grabs the current screen of every session and files it as a candidate case costs almost nothing and gives us real material every day.
  • Last night. The limit-until-3am screens and the API-error screens are the two most valuable cases we have and they have already happened. Worth checking what we still hold before it ages out.
  • You, as you hit them. When a session stops and you deal with it by hand, that is a case. The cheapest capture is a command you run at that moment.

What the harness reports

One table, every case, per model: what it answered, whether that was right, how long it took. Plus the one number that matters most - wrong answers on the negative cases. And it must run against the real engine, not a copy of it, or it will certify something that is not what ships.

4. The plan

STEP 1

Build the harness and fill it. The case format, the runner, the capture command, and every case we can get - your four, plus negatives for each, plus whatever the fleet is showing right now. Nothing else changes. The output is a table that says which of your real problems the engine can and cannot handle, measured rather than argued.

STEP 2

Fix the model choice, which the harness will have settled. Today's evaluating call asks a reasoning model to write six hundred characters of JSON and times out about one time in three. The replacement is a yes/no question answered in 0.4 seconds by the cheap model, with the text to type decided when you wrote the rule and approved by you - which is safer as well as faster, because nothing is composed at 3am that you have never seen.

This is small and it is the difference between a feature that works one time in three and one that works.

STEP 3

The clock. Your most common case. A rule needs to be able to say "not now - come back at 3am", which means something has to wake a session that is not doing anything. That is a scheduled re-look, and DevThrottle already runs schedules.

This is the single highest-value gap, because it is the one that had everything sitting there all night.

STEP 4

Actions that are not one keystroke. The login case needs a sequence, and two of its steps reach off the screen - a browser, and your email.

One correction to what you said, and it makes this cheaper than it sounds: the rules engine does not have to move into the Director. It already works this way - the Gateway decides and the Director acts, because that is how the typing gets done. What is missing is not a home for the engine, it is vocabulary: Director-side steps we write and review, like "open this link" and "read the newest sign-in code out of the email". The decision stays where it is. And these are our code, never the model's - exactly the ruling that is already settled.

STEP 5

The colour. Decide whether "only tell me when it actually needs me" is a product fix or a rule. My view is above: fix it centrally, and let a rule be the exception. Worth doing after the harness has a few real red-but-did-not-need-you screens in it, because that is the evidence for whether the distinction is even reliably readable.

5. What I would like you to decide

  • Is this the right order? Harness first, then the model fix, then the clock. The clock is your most painful case, so there is an argument for it going second.
  • How do we get real screens? The fastest is a capture command you run when a session stops on something. Would you use that, or should it capture automatically from the fleet?
  • Anything still on disk from last night? Those two incidents are the best cases we will ever have and I do not want to reconstruct them from memory.
